# Future Skills Advisor: Skills Guidance Notes

Document type: Team-written guidance notes. This is NOT a research report and NOT a source of statistics.
Audience: Undergraduate students at any university.
Purpose: Help the assistant turn a student's interview answers into clear, practical skill-development priorities.

## How the assistant should use these notes

- Use these notes for HOW to build skills, how to prioritise, and how to adapt advice to a student's field of study, interests, available time and learning style.
- Do not use these notes as evidence about the job market. Claims about demand, growth, or what employers expect must come from the uploaded reports and be cited by report name and page.
- If the uploaded reports do not cover a point, say so clearly instead of guessing.
- These notes contain no university course information. Never recommend specific courses, degree programs, or instructors. Recommend skills, practice types, and project ideas instead.
- Recommendations are guidance, not guarantees. Students should check them against their own goals.

# Part 1: Skill categories

These twelve categories are the framework used in this guide. Each section can be read on its own.

### Skill: AI literacy and working with AI tools

- What it is: Understanding what AI tools can and cannot do, using them effectively for study and work, checking their output, and knowing when not to use them.
- Signs you already have it: You use AI tools for study or projects and verify the answers instead of copying them.
- How to build it: Choose one routine task (summarising readings, drafting, debugging code) and use an AI tool on it for a month. Keep a log of where the tool was wrong. Compare two tools on the same task. Learn basic prompt writing and basic privacy rules, such as what not to paste into a tool.
- First step: Start an "AI mistakes log" and note every wrong or doubtful answer for two weeks.

### Skill: Data literacy and analytical thinking

- What it is: Reading, questioning and using data. This includes cleaning simple datasets, making charts, understanding averages and trends, and spotting misleading numbers.
- Signs you already have it: You are comfortable with spreadsheets and ask where numbers come from.
- How to build it: Analyse a small public dataset about a topic you care about, make three clear charts, and write one page about what the data does and does not show. Practise spreadsheet skills first, then a basic data tool or programming language.
- First step: Pick one public dataset and write three questions it could answer.

### Skill: Critical thinking and problem solving

- What it is: Breaking a vague problem into parts, weighing evidence, testing assumptions, and choosing a reasoned solution.
- Signs you already have it: You can explain why you chose one option over another.
- How to build it: Take a real problem (for example a campus or community issue), define it in one sentence, list possible causes, gather evidence, and propose and compare two solutions. Practise explaining your reasoning to others and ask them to challenge it.
- First step: Write a one-page problem brief with the problem, evidence, and two possible solutions.

### Skill: Creative thinking and innovation

- What it is: Generating original ideas, combining ideas from different areas, and turning ideas into something testable.
- Signs you already have it: You often suggest new ways of doing things and like to experiment.
- How to build it: Join a hackathon, design challenge, or pitch competition. Practise idea generation with time limits, then build a quick prototype or mock-up and get feedback. Keep an ideas notebook.
- First step: Set a 20-minute timer, write ten ideas for one problem, then pick one to prototype.

### Skill: Communication and storytelling

- What it is: Explaining ideas clearly in writing, speech and visuals, tailoring the message to the audience, and presenting evidence in a persuasive but honest way.
- Signs you already have it: Others understand your explanations without long follow-up questions.
- How to build it: Write short articles or project summaries, give presentations to friends or clubs, and practise turning a technical result into a story with a clear problem, finding and next step. Ask for feedback each time.
- First step: Explain one idea from your studies in 90 seconds to someone outside your field.

### Skill: Collaboration and leadership

- What it is: Working well in teams, sharing responsibility, giving and receiving feedback, resolving conflict, and guiding others toward a goal.
- Signs you already have it: Teammates trust you to follow through and you help the group make decisions.
- How to build it: Take a role in a student club, team project or volunteer group. Practise running meetings with an agenda, assigning clear tasks, and reviewing what went well afterwards.
- First step: In your next group project, offer to write the task list and check in on progress each week.

### Skill: Adaptability and learning how to learn

- What it is: Staying curious, picking up new tools quickly, handling change and setbacks, and keeping a regular learning habit.
- Signs you already have it: You try new tools without fear and bounce back from mistakes.
- How to build it: Choose one new tool or topic each month and set a small goal with a deadline. Write a short reflection about what worked. Seek feedback early and treat mistakes as information.
- First step: Pick one small skill to learn in four weeks and schedule fixed learning times in your calendar.

### Skill: Technical and digital foundations

- What it is: Core digital skills such as advanced spreadsheet use, basic programming, version control, working with documents and collaboration tools, and understanding how software and data systems work.
- Signs you already have it: You automate repeated tasks and learn new software quickly.
- How to build it: Learn one programming language at a basic level through a small project, such as a tool that processes a file or a simple web page. Use version control for your work and keep your files organised.
- First step: Automate one boring task you do every week, even if it is just with a spreadsheet formula or a short script.

### Skill: Domain knowledge and cross-disciplinary thinking

- What it is: Deep knowledge in your own field combined with enough understanding of other fields (for example technology, business, design or science) to work with experts there.
- Signs you already have it: You can explain how your field connects to another one.
- How to build it: Read about how your field is changing, take on a project that mixes two fields, and talk to people who work in a different area. Keep strengthening your main subject, because deep knowledge makes your use of general skills more valuable.
- First step: Write down three ways technology or data could change a problem in your field.

### Skill: Responsible technology use (ethics, privacy and security awareness)

- What it is: Understanding fairness, bias, privacy, intellectual property and basic security when using AI and data, and making responsible choices.
- Signs you already have it: You think about who could be harmed or left out by a tool or decision.
- How to build it: When you do a project, add a short section on risks, bias, privacy and how you would reduce them. Learn basic account and data security habits. Read case studies of technology that went wrong and discuss what could have been done differently.
- First step: Add a "risks and limits" paragraph to your next assignment or project.

### Skill: Self-management and project management

- What it is: Planning your time, setting priorities, meeting deadlines, tracking progress and finishing what you start.
- Signs you already have it: You break big tasks into steps and rarely miss deadlines.
- How to build it: Use a simple task board, set weekly goals, and review at the end of each week. Run one personal project from idea to completion, with milestones and a final summary.
- First step: Put your next big assignment on a simple board with three columns: to do, doing and done.

### Skill: Language and cross-cultural skills

- What it is: Communicating across languages and cultures, including professional English, and working effectively with people from different backgrounds.
- Signs you already have it: You can follow and join discussions in more than one language or cultural setting.
- How to build it: Practise professional writing and speaking in English, join international or exchange activities, and work with international teammates on projects. Learn to adjust your style for different audiences.
- First step: Write one professional email or short report in English each week and ask for feedback.

# Part 2: Guidance by field of study

The labels below match the options in the student interview.

### If the student studies: Business/Economics

- Strengths to build on: understanding of markets, decisions, and organisations.
- Skills to add first: data literacy, AI literacy, communication and storytelling.
- Useful project ideas: analyse a company's public data and recommend one improvement; build a simple dashboard in a spreadsheet; write a short case study on how AI changes a business process.

### If the student studies: Engineering

- Strengths to build on: problem solving, technical depth, and structured thinking.
- Skills to add first: communication, collaboration and leadership, AI literacy, responsible technology use.
- Useful project ideas: build a small prototype and document it for non-experts; use data from a sensor or public source to improve a design; lead a team build and write up lessons learned.

### If the student studies: Science/Math/Statistics

- Strengths to build on: analytical thinking, comfort with data and models, careful reasoning.
- Skills to add first: communication and storytelling, technical foundations (programming and version control), cross-disciplinary thinking.
- Useful project ideas: reproduce an analysis from a published paper; turn a technical result into a short article or a clear chart for a general audience; apply a statistical method to a real community dataset.

### If the student studies: Humanities/Communication

- Strengths to build on: writing, interpretation, cultural understanding and communication.
- Skills to add first: data literacy, AI literacy, digital foundations, critical thinking about AI-generated content.
- Useful project ideas: analyse text data from a public source and write about the findings; run a communication project and track results with simple numbers; test AI writing tools and write a critical review of their strengths and weaknesses.

### If the student studies: Social Sciences

- Strengths to build on: understanding people and society, research methods, critical analysis.
- Skills to add first: data literacy, AI literacy, communication of findings, responsible technology use.
- Useful project ideas: design and run a small survey and analyse it; study how a technology affects a community; build a short data story using public statistics.

### If the student studies: Health

- Strengths to build on: care for people, evidence-based thinking, and responsibility.
- Skills to add first: data literacy, AI literacy, communication, responsible technology use (privacy matters especially).
- Useful project ideas: analyse a public health dataset; explain a health topic clearly to non-experts; review how digital tools are used in health settings and list risks and benefits.

### If the student studies: Design/Creative

- Strengths to build on: creativity, visual communication, understanding users.
- Skills to add first: AI literacy for creative work, data literacy (user research), collaboration, technical foundations.
- Useful project ideas: build a portfolio piece that uses AI tools and describe what you changed by hand; run a small user test and improve a design from the results; collaborate with a student developer on a prototype.

### If the student studies: Other

- Strengths to build on: whatever is distinctive in the student's field.
- Skills to add first: AI literacy, data literacy, communication and adaptability, since these are useful across fields.
- Useful project ideas: pick one problem in your own field and explore how data or AI tools could help; document the work in a short portfolio page.

# Part 3: Guidance by career direction

The labels below match the options in the student interview.

### If the student is interested in: Data & AI

- Skills to prioritise: data literacy, technical foundations (programming), AI literacy, responsible technology use, communication of results.
- Build path: spreadsheet skills, then a programming language, then small data projects with clear questions, then a simple model with an honest evaluation.
- Project ideas: predict or explain something in a public dataset and describe the limits; build a small tool that uses an AI model for a real need; write a short report on bias risks in your own project.

### If the student is interested in: Software/Tech

- Skills to prioritise: technical foundations, problem solving, collaboration, AI literacy for coding assistance, adaptability.
- Build path: one language to a basic level, version control, small projects that run end to end, then teamwork on a shared codebase.
- Project ideas: a small web or mobile tool for a student club; contribute a small fix to an open project; build something with an AI assistant and document what you checked and fixed yourself.

### If the student is interested in: Business/Management

- Skills to prioritise: communication, collaboration and leadership, data literacy, self-management, AI literacy.
- Build path: lead small groups, practise decision making with data, learn to present recommendations clearly.
- Project ideas: run a club event with a budget and report on results; analyse a business case and present to a mock board; build a simple plan using AI tools for research and check their claims.

### If the student is interested in: Marketing/Communication

- Skills to prioritise: communication and storytelling, creative thinking, data literacy for audience analysis, AI literacy.
- Build path: create content regularly, learn to measure what works, use AI tools for drafts while keeping your own voice and checking facts.
- Project ideas: run a small content campaign for a club and track simple results; write a case study on how AI changed a marketing task; build a portfolio of short pieces.

### If the student is interested in: Creative/Design

- Skills to prioritise: creative thinking, communication, AI literacy for creative tools, collaboration, responsible technology use (copyright and originality).
- Build path: build a portfolio, learn from feedback, try AI tools as part of the process, and keep a record of your own decisions.
- Project ideas: a design project with a user test; a portfolio piece that blends AI-assisted work and hand-made work with notes on both; a collaborative project with students from another field.

### If the student is interested in: Research/Academia

- Skills to prioritise: critical thinking, data literacy, communication, technical foundations, self-management.
- Build path: read widely in one topic, learn to question methods, practise writing clearly, and join a research group or project.
- Project ideas: write a short literature review; reproduce a small published analysis; present a poster at a student event.

### If the student is interested in: Entrepreneurship

- Skills to prioritise: creative thinking, communication, collaboration and leadership, data literacy, self-management, adaptability.
- Build path: test small ideas quickly, talk to real users, learn from failures and track simple numbers.
- Project ideas: run a small experiment to see whether people want a product or service; build a landing page or prototype with AI tools and gather feedback; pitch an idea and write down what you learned.

### If the student is interested in: Not sure yet

- Approach: Do not push a career. Help the student explore. Start with the activity answers and build a flexible foundation of skills that are useful in many directions: AI literacy, data literacy, communication, critical thinking and adaptability.
- Suggested step: Try three small projects in different directions over one semester, then note which one gave the most energy and which one felt like a chore.

### Mapping the activities a student enjoys to skills

- Analyzing data: data literacy, critical thinking, technical foundations.
- Building things: technical foundations, problem solving, creative thinking.
- Talking with people: communication, collaboration and leadership, language and cross-cultural skills.
- Creating content: creative thinking, communication and storytelling, AI literacy.
- Solving puzzles: critical thinking, problem solving, technical foundations.
- Organizing projects: self-management and project management, collaboration and leadership.

# Part 4: Guidance by time available per week

### If the student has: Under 2 hours per week

- Choose one skill only. Use short, regular sessions of 20 to 30 minutes, 4 times a week.
- Pick small first steps from the skill sections, such as the AI mistakes log or a 90-second explanation.
- Combine learning with existing coursework when possible, for example use class projects to practise one skill.

### If the student has: 2-5 hours per week

- Choose one main skill and one small supporting skill.
- Aim for one small project over four to six weeks, with a weekly review.
- Keep one session per week for practice and one for reflection or feedback.

### If the student has: 5-10 hours per week

- Choose two skills and run one project that uses both of them.
- Include a collaborative element, such as a study group, club or team project.
- Reserve time each month to show your work to someone and ask for feedback.

### If the student has: 10+ hours per week

- Build a three-part plan: a main skill, a supporting skill, and a portfolio project.
- Protect rest and study time. Do not try to learn everything at once. Review progress every month and change the plan if needed.
- Share your work publicly or with mentors, such as a portfolio page or a short write-up.

# Part 5: Guidance by learning style

### If the student prefers: Hands-on projects

- Turn every skill into a small project with a clear result. Keep a project log and a portfolio page.
- Start small and finish things, because completed small projects teach more than unfinished big ones.

### If the student prefers: Videos/online lessons

- Use online lessons for the basics, but add a practice task after every lesson so knowledge turns into skill.
- Limit yourself to one lesson series at a time and finish it before starting another.

### If the student prefers: Reading

- Read a mix of practical guides and reports, and write a short summary of each in your own words.
- Pair reading with one application task per week so the ideas are tested.

### If the student prefers: With friends/study groups

- Form a small group with a shared goal, a weekly meeting, and clear roles.
- Teach each other: explaining a topic to others is a strong way to learn it.

### If the student prefers: With a mentor

- Look for a senior student, a teacher, a club leader, or a professional willing to give feedback.
- Come to each meeting with a specific question and a short update on what you did since the last meeting.

# Part 6: Responding to common worries

### If the student's biggest worry is: AI replacing jobs

- Acknowledge the worry as understandable. Do not promise that jobs are safe and do not claim that they will disappear.
- Point to evidence from the uploaded reports about how jobs and skills are changing, and cite them.
- Give actions: learn to use AI tools well, build skills that work with AI such as judgement, communication and domain knowledge, and keep learning.

### If the student's biggest worry is: Not knowing which skills to build

- Reduce the problem: choose three skills at most, based on the student's field, interests and strengths.
- Suggest a short experiment: spend four weeks on one skill and review whether it was useful and enjoyable.

### If the student's biggest worry is: Lack of experience

- Explain that experience can come from projects, clubs, volunteering, competitions and personal work, not only from jobs.
- Suggest a small project that produces something to show, and a way to describe it in a CV or portfolio.

### If the student's biggest worry is: Too many options

- Help the student narrow down: pick one direction for one semester and one main skill.
- Use the rule: choose, try, review. After the trial period, change the plan if needed.

### If the student's biggest worry is: Keeping up with change

- Suggest a simple, sustainable habit: a weekly learning time, a short list of trusted sources, and a monthly review.
- Emphasise learning how to learn, because specific tools change quickly and the ability to pick up new ones lasts.

# Part 7: How to prioritise skills (Now, Next, Later)

### Planning framework

- Now (the next 1 to 3 months): foundations that make other learning easier, such as AI literacy, data literacy basics, and a weekly learning habit. Choose small tasks that can be finished.
- Next (3 to 12 months): the core skills linked to the student's chosen direction, practised through one or two real projects.
- Later (beyond 1 year): deeper specialisation, leadership roles, internships or larger portfolio projects.

### Principles for good recommendations

- Link each recommended skill to something the student said in the interview.
- Recommend three to five skills, not more.
- For each skill give a reason, a first action, and a way to know it is working.
- Prefer a mix of one technical skill and one human skill, such as data literacy with communication.
- Be honest about uncertainty and tell the student to check the evidence in the reports.

# Part 8: Project ideas library

### Small projects (2 to 4 weeks)

- Keep an AI mistakes log and write a one-page summary of what you learned.
- Analyse one public dataset and make three charts with a short written conclusion.
- Explain a hard topic from your field in a 3-minute presentation and record feedback.
- Automate one repeated task with a spreadsheet formula or short script.

### Medium projects (1 to 3 months)

- Build a small tool or dashboard that solves a problem for a student club.
- Run a survey, analyse the results, and present the findings.
- Create a portfolio page showing three finished pieces with short descriptions of what you did and learned.
- Join a hackathon, case competition or community project in a team.

### Larger projects (a semester or more)

- Lead a team project from idea to delivery with milestones and a final reflection.
- Do a research or capstone-style project that combines your field with data or AI tools, and write about its limits.
- Build a long-term portfolio with a record of how you used AI tools responsibly.

# Part 9: Limits of this guide

- These notes give general guidance and do not replace personal career advice from advisors, teachers or professionals.
- These notes do not include information about any university's courses, programs or instructors, so they cannot recommend them.
- These notes do not contain statistics or predictions about the job market. Evidence about skills in demand comes only from the uploaded reports and must be cited by report name and page.
- Job markets and tools change quickly. Students should check the most recent versions of the reports and update their plans regularly.

# Glossary

- AI literacy: knowing what AI tools do, how to use them well, and how to judge their output.
- Data literacy: the ability to read, question and use data to support decisions.
- Upskilling: learning new skills to improve in your current direction.
- Reskilling: learning new skills to move into a different direction.
- Portfolio: a collection of your work that shows what you can do.
- Cross-disciplinary: combining knowledge from more than one field.
- Learning how to learn: the habit and strategies that let you pick up new skills quickly.
